Resolving Question III.5 of Stepanov–Herrmann–Guhr (arXiv:2104.08966v1, p. 7): the top-eigenvector weight gap occurs exactly above sigma = c

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Correlation matrices describe how quantities vary together, but their strongest spectral direction need not align most closely with all quantities moving together. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang determine when prescribed average correlation and spread allow this mismatch in every sufficiently large dimension. In the positive-mean region left open by Stepanov, Herrmann and Guhr, their answer is that the spread must strictly exceed the mean.

A correlation matrix is a real symmetric array with diagonal entries one and nonnegative eigenvalues. Its off-diagonal entries describe pairwise correlations; their mean is c and their standard deviation is σ. Eigenvectors are perpendicular directions that the matrix simply stretches, with eigenvalues giving the stretching factors. The reference direction has equal positive coordinates and length one. An eigenvector’s weight is its squared overlap with this direction; the weights sum to one. The required gap means that, in every orthonormal eigenbasis, each largest-eigenvalue vector has smaller weight than some other basis vector. Finding one convenient basis is not enough.

Stepanov, Herrmann and Guhr had established impossibility and existence in different regions, leaving their Question III.5 between them. Ma and Zhang settle that remaining region and extend the classification to all admissible fixed parameter pairs. “Eventual existence” means that, beyond a threshold depending on the pair, every integer dimension admits an example with exactly the prescribed moments. Their complete condition is 0 ≤ c < 1/2, σ > c, and c² + σ² < 1. This asserts existence, not that every matrix with those moments has the gap.

The obstruction links entrywise averages to spectral information. Subtracting one from each eigenvalue makes their sum zero. Their squared sum records the correlations’ mean and variance, while their weighted average records the positive mean. A gap forces the top weight below one half, so this weighted average cannot exceed the average of the two largest centered eigenvalues. Those two must therefore be sufficiently large, while the remaining eigenvalues must balance their sum. Bounding the resulting squared sum forces σ > c in each finite dimension. Nonnegative eigenvalues separately exclude c ≥ 1/2. For c > 0 and σ ≤ c, the authors also prove that the leading direction is unique and has weight above one half.

For existence, the authors split coordinates into two groups. They add positive multiples of the identity and two outer products, formed from the all-ones vector and a vector with opposite signs on the groups. This ensures positive definiteness and constant within-group and between-group correlations, a block family previously studied by Roustant and Deville. Choosing group proportions and adjusting coefficients after rounding group sizes preserves c and σ exactly. Only two eigendirections have nonzero weights. The parameters put the reference direction’s weighted eigenvalue below their midpoint, forcing the larger eigenvalue’s weight below one half and the smaller eigenvalue’s weight above it.

A concrete example has sixteen coordinates, split into groups of twelve and four. Distinct coordinates in the same group have correlation 11/12; those in different groups have correlation −5/12. The mean is 23/60 and the variance is 32/75. The two active eigenvectors have weights 3/7 and 4/7, with the smaller weight attached to the larger eigenvalue. All other weights vanish: strongest amplification and closest alignment with equal-coordinate motion are genuinely different.

Repeated eigenvalues matter because rotating a basis can concentrate or spread overlap within an eigenspace. The authors’ exact criterion compares the top eigenspace’s total overlap with the average overlap per dimension in each lower eigenspace. On the boundary c² + σ² = 1, correlation matrices are signed rank-one matrices; the universal gap holds exactly when c < 0. These finite-dimensional examples do not contradict the eventual classification: a fixed negative mean becomes incompatible with positive semidefiniteness as dimension increases. For zero mean, the construction works for every fixed 0 < σ < 1.

The manuscript gives written proofs for these conclusions. Its reported finite computations check selected identities and examples, not the quantifier over all sufficiently large dimensions. The stated Lean evidence covers the equivalence answering Question III.5 on its remaining domain; the broader classification, eigenspace criterion, boundary results and other refinements have separate written proofs, outside that declaration’s formal scope. The result precisely locates when mean and spread permit this alignment reversal, without classifying every small dimension. Higher-moment refinements involving skewness and kurtosis, raised in the earlier work, are not addressed.
